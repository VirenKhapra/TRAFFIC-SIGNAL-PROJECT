How to read this

What each number means

  • Average wait: how long a typical car sits in the queue. This is the main result.
  • Cars waiting: how many cars are queued at any moment, across all four roads.
  • Cars served: how many got through. It is nearly equal for every sensible signal because the road has spare capacity, so the difference is how soon cars get through.
  • Signal changes: how often the light switches. Each change loses 5 s to yellow, so changing too often wastes time. That is why Longest-Queue loses to the agent.

Why you see 42%, 38% and 36%

  • 42% is the project result: 16.8 s to 9.7 s, averaged over the 20 test hours.
  • 38% comes from a wider check of 200 other hours with the same saved agent: 16.3 s to 10.1 s.
  • 36% is one single hour, the one demo.py prints and this page opens with: 15.0 s to 9.6 s.

Each hour has different traffic, so the percentage moves. The agent wins clearly in all three.

How the agent learned

  • What it sees: how busy each road is, which road has green, and how long it has been green. That makes 288 possible situations.
  • What it can do: keep the green or switch. A green must last at least 10 s.
  • How it is scored: a penalty for every car waiting, at every 5 s step.
  • Practice: 200 simulated hours of trial and error, ending in a table of scores for each situation. That table drives the live demo above.

Limits of this result

  • This is a simulation of one junction with made-up demand. It has not been tested on real traffic.
  • The agent is usually steady but sometimes has a bad hour. Across 1,000 simulated hours the typical wait was 9.7 s, while the worst single hour reached 43.7 s.
  • Q-Learning and SARSA are effectively tied. In steady traffic SARSA is slightly ahead.
